# Fair Play

## Overview

This is a web application based on Eve Rodsky's book, Fair Play. Fair Play is a book written in response to the common problem of women ending up saddled with the vast majority of the household and child care work, even in more progressive families. Rodsky herself has a Harvard Law degree, but like so many other women, including many highly accomplished ones, she left the workforce for a time to take care of children. During this time, she noticed just how much of what she calls the invisible labor fell on her. And when women are working outside of the home, the burden is even greater on them. Many women, in addition to their full-time jobs, have what's been called a second shift when they come home, which involves everything from taking care of the children to taking out the trash, walking the dog, etc. By contrast, their husbands often come home after a busy day of work, plop themselves on the couch, and play video games or watch TV. They also expect their wives to have dinner on the table, even if their wives themselves also have full-time jobs. Fair Play was written to address this fundamental unfairness. It proposes a better system of more equitably distributing domestic chores between both partners. 

The premise of Fair Play is that there are a hundred cards which have to do with every domestic or household task under the sun. Many of these cards involve soul-sucking tasks, such as:
- Preparing school lunches for kids
- Picking up dog poop
- Taking kids to the dentist, etc.

But by contrast, some of the cards are more emotional or intangible in nature such as comforting a kid after a nightmare or taking time as a couple to go on a date night without the kids. And there are even a couple of what Rodsky calls "unicorn cards," which are essentially cards where each partner gets to pursue passions, and keep in touch with parts of their identity that are often lost when you have kids. 

Partners are supposed to first have a discussion about their values and what each of them wants from their partnership and what each of them sees as the role they want to have in the household. Then partners are supposed to divide up the cards in an equitable manner. The ultimate result is supposed to be a more sustainable and fair system where each partner contributes and plays to the household but also has work that they feel is a fair load relative to what the other partner carries. Partners can change cards over time as well. 

This web application will mirror and re-create this card system. The application is meant for couples who will take digital versions of cards and assign them among themselves. There will be an accountability system in which partners can mark the cards that they have finished or the cards that they've not been able to get around to doing. I'm envisioning some sort of scoring system where the more equitable the load, the higher the couple's overall score. And it would be a sort of gamified system where a couple is going for the highest possible combined score. For instance, if Bill has 60 cards and Susie has 40, or the reverse, the system would be largely but not entirely equitable. But each Core Card might also have a certain weight to it, since not all cards are created equal. For instance, a heavy task such as handling health insurance or preparing school lunches every day would probably have a higher score than something like picking up the newspaper every morning. 

You, my AI assistant, will help me build this application.

## The application flow

I'm envisioning a flow similar to the following for each couple. This flow is based on Rodsky's book, particularly Chapter 8. We can take some liberties with her methodology. For instance, as you'll see below, I've changed up some of the steps' order slightly, but in general we should follow the spirit of what she envisions as the fair play system and how it should be executed. 
1. Each member of a couple logs into the account. They each have separate logins, but it will be a joint account. One member of the couple creates the account and invites the other to join. I'm envisioning individual logins, but for the MVP, which I envision is just being used by my wife and me, we might have a simpler system. For example, there might be something like a Google SSO OIDC where each of us signs in, and then we can use the application from one platform to another. 
2. Once each partner signs in, there will be a questionnaire that each partner has to answer. Couples are encouraged to do this questionnaire jointly, but if they want to, they can also do it separately. The questionnaire will cover the values that the couple holds, which will be important in dividing up the cards. Each partner will also say things that they're good at (aptitudes). For example, if a partner has an MBA or a finance degree, then handling the family finances and budgeting might be a logical job for them. But by the same token, it's important to realize that most of these jobs aren't going to be fun. Partners should be honest about their own abilities, but also realize that most of these cards are not going to fall into their wheelhouse in terms of what they trained in. For example, going back to the prior case, somebody with an MBA is mostly going to have to do chores, have to do tasks. They have nothing to do with business or finance. Each partner should also mention anything in their background that might predispose them to a particular card or set of cards. For example, if they've worked on fixing up cars as a hobby since they were a teenager, then maybe the car and maintenance-related tasks should go to them. Finally, and most importantly, the initial survey should ask whether the couple has kids. This is critical because if they don't have kids, then dozens of these cards are not relevant. The application should take this information about whether they have kids and, based on that, decide which cards are relevant to the couple and throw all the others out.  
3. Now, for the first time that members of the club will actually see all the cards. This first step: each member of the couple should mark the card that they're currently holding. This step should not be about aspiration or what should be the case. This step purely reflects what's the case right now. Each member of the couple marks the card that they're holding, and the application remembers that. Note that some cards can be handled by both members of the couple, and some cards might only be done partially. That's okay. For now, if you do any part of the task of a card, mark yourself on it. 
4. Then we get to the meat of the application, which is actually selecting cards.  The cards that are available need to mimic the 100 cards that Rodsky describes in her book. Each partner individually will look at all the cards and rank them as follows:
a. Whether the partner considers the card essential to the function of the household
b. OR important to the function of the household but something that can be skipped occasionally
c. OR something that's only important maybe half the time
d. OR something that can be skipped or only done very occasionally
Then, the computer will tally up the scores, and the partners should, as a couple, look back at the cards and see where each partner ranked them. At this point, the couple jointly will need to make a final decision on which cards are essential, not so essential, or can be skipped. We should try to follow Rodsky's methodology as much as possible on this, as in the other parts of the app. 
5. Then, the partners evaluate each card based on Rodsky's schema of minimum standards of care (MSC). This means that for each card that the two of you decide are important enough to keep in your deck, you need to describe the minimum standard for completing that task in an acceptable manner. For example, to take a relatively simple task: taking out the garbage. A minimum standard of care might be something like the following:
- The kitchen trash can sit around for no more than two full days.
- Other trash bins that don't have as much organic trash can sit around for no longer than five days.
- OR, whenever any trash can reaches the top, the person holding the card must promptly empty it into the large trash bin outside. (You can see, even with a simple example, how subjective MSC can be.)
6. Then, the partners should decide on what constitutes CPE for each card. CPE is what Rodzki described as Conception Plan Execution. It's how each card will actually be implemented. The criteria for successful CPE are based on the minimum standards of care that the couple just agreed on. A CPE should be everything required to take meeting the MSC from hypothetical to completed. Using our trash example, conception might be that the cardholder realizes that, "Oh crap, the kitchen trash is almost full. It should probably be taken out." Planning is the steps to take out the trash, which sounds simple, except that it actually involves a few steps:
a. Tying up the existing trash bag
b. Taking it out of the trash can in the kitchen
c. Replacing the kitchen trash can with a fresh bag
d. Then taking the bag full of trash outside and putting it in the main trash can or dumpster.
7. Finally, decide on what Rodsky calls each partner's unicorn space. This is the space each partner gets to devote to their own unique, specific interests and passions. These are often passions that each member of the couple had before having kids and that they feel have been lost or largely lost after becoming parents. But they could also be new interests that have been developed since becoming parents. For example, maybe a mother decides that she really wants to run a marathon. Either way, the unicorn space is something that belongs to each member of the couple, and it's essential that each partner choose this.
8. Once the unicorn space is decided, the partners work on the cards they have chosen. Here, different people and different couples will do things differently. Some people might find it valuable to tick off every single card that they've done every time they do it, whereas others find that's too laborious. Sometimes it's enough to just see the cards as they're there and know that each partner is responsible for their own cards. But there will be the option for each partner to click cards as completed once they're done. Ultimately, whether each partner clicks to complete the cards as they're done or later on, the cards that are done will need to be marked as completed so the program can keep track of who's completed what. Beyond the MVP, we might allow partners to also click off each part of C, P, or E as they're done as well. This could even be integrated in the MVP if feasible. 
9. After periodic intervals, the couple will be able to reassign cards. The application will have a default period of two weeks between reassignments, but this can be adjusted to a time interval that the couple wants. On each reassignment, the couple can decide to change cards with each other as they see fit.

## Scoring

The application will use a scoring system to determine how well each partner is keeping up with their cards and how equitable the overall arrangement is. Each card will have an equitability score, which details how complex or taxing a given card is. The most taxing cards are what Rodsky calls the non-negotiables or daily grind. These cards are generally unpleasant in nature, often have to occur at a specific time, and are typically time-sensitive. Many of them are also cards that are especially disproportionately handed to women. Taking on and completing these cards gives a partner a higher score. And the application will be able to keep track of the different scores. It's important, though, that a concern is that this might encourage bean counting among the couples, among the members of the couple. That is, "Oh, I got 450 points this week. You only did 320, and so you need to start carrying your weight." You need to try to figure out a way to minimize this in the scoring system. It's possible that scoring may be left off entirely, at least in the MVP, to focus on the basic mechanics of cards. The scoring system is something that the human and A.I. assistant will have to work together to resolve, and it might require multiple iterations. But in theory, the main virtue of a scoring system is that it would help to enforce equitability among the partners. Also, it would help to enforce accountability, that is, ensuring that somebody who took on a card actually did it. 

## AI 

If possible, I would like to integrate AI into this application. This might come after the MVP. The AI could, for instance, learn over time who takes on what card, and so it might offer suggestions as to which card each partner should take on. For example, if Sarah is always taking on a particularly tough card such as diaper changes, then maybe after two or three cycles, the AI suggests that her partner Dan take this on. One issue is that the AI itself will be biased because it will have trained on material that generally will be biased towards stereotypical gender roles. This will require some inventive countermeasures. For example, giving a particularly detailed system prompt in which you specify that something like "You are a fair and equitable AI assistant that helps couples manage their cards. You are strongly committed to gender equity..." will help to mitigate this bias.
